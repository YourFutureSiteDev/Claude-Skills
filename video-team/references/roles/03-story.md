# Role 3 — Story

**Method sources:** two independent teardowns of Ryan Trahan's work. Trent
Haire's structural breakdown of the first video in the penny series
(`creatorcampfire.co/post/how-ryan-trahan-hooks-you-with-story`), which is where
the five building blocks below come from, and George Blackman's technique
breakdown of "I Spent 100 Days In Grand Theft Auto"
(`georgeblackman.substack.com/p/13-unexpectedly-effective-youtube`). Trahan's own
account of why the penny format works is on the YouTube creator blog
(`blog.youtube/creator-and-artist-stories/ryan-trahan-thumbnail-editing-secrets-to-going-viral/`),
where he makes the point that high production value is not what makes something
connect.

**Produces:** `beats[]` and the `words` budget.

Method, not voice.

## The five building blocks

Haire's framework, and the most useful part of either source, because it gives
you a way to audit a script line by line. Every second of a video belongs in one
of five buckets:

| Block | What it is |
|---|---|
| **Value delivery** | the thing the viewer actually came for, judged from their side |
| **Progression** | the vehicle carrying them toward the next value delivery |
| **Context** | why they should care, so they never feel lost |
| **Disruption** | the jolt that stops attention drifting |
| **CTA** | anything you take rather than give: like, follow, buy, donate |

And a sixth bucket that is the point of the whole exercise:

**Useless.** If a segment does not fit one of the five, it is almost certainly
dead weight from the viewer's side. The named offenders are lingering,
repetition, irrelevant context, CTAs placed before any value, and repetition
again. Haire's audit of Trahan's own video flags two moments where a total is
read out after it has already been shown on screen: no new information, so no
reason to be there.

**Applied here, this is the audit.** After role 4 writes the script, label every
sentence with one of the six. Any sentence labelled useless gets cut, and the
words it frees go back into the budget. On a 30-second short there is room for
about twenty sentences, so one wasted sentence is 5% of the video.

## Constrain the goal to open the loop

Blackman's clearest observation. The GTA video's stated aim, earning enough to
buy the most expensive condo, is not interesting on its own. The constraint makes
it interesting: do it legally. Suddenly the goal looks impossible and the viewer
needs to know how it resolves.

Applied here: an angle without a constraint has no tension. "I built a website"
is nothing. "I built a website without writing any code, in an hour, for
nothing" has three constraints and each one is a reason to keep watching. Add the
constraint at this stage if role 1 did not.

## Context immediately, then never again

In the GTA video the rules arrive up front: how long a day in the game is, how
long he will play, what the goal is. The viewer is never confused about what they
are watching, because confusion is when people leave.

Haire's rules for context: keep it snappy, keep it relevant, keep it necessary.

Applied to 30 seconds: context is **one sentence**, and it comes second, right
after the hook. Any more and the video is all setup. If the idea cannot be
explained in one sentence, role 1 handed over a topic rather than an angle.

## Make the clock visible

The GTA video pins dollars to a wall to represent time passing, including while
he sleeps. By the time the video is 60% through he has under 10% of the money,
and that visible gap is what holds the viewer: they want the tension resolved.

Applied here: the progress of a short should be visible on screen, not just
spoken. A counter, a total, a bar, a number that moves. This is a note for role
5's shot list as much as for this one, so write it into the beat.

## Escalate, and let one moment breathe

Two pacing notes that pull against each other usefully.

Blackman's three-act read of the GTA video is a genuine arc: the goal is pursued
legally, then the rules start slipping, then it is achieved and the cost is
visible. Something changes in the person, not just the total.

But he also notes that Trahan deliberately breaks the constant-motion rule:
after finally buying a first car, he drives around doing nothing for a while.
Because the viewer is rooting for him by then, the pause lets them enjoy it with
him. Sometimes it pays to let a moment hang.

Applied to 30 seconds: you get **one** breath, and it goes immediately after the
payoff, not in the middle. Everything before the payoff escalates.

## Every beat changes the situation

The test that decides whether you have beats or a list. After each beat, what is
true that was not true before? If the answer is "the viewer knows one more
fact", it is a list item. Merge it or cut it.

A list has no reason to be watched past the first item. An escalation does.

## Recurring motifs, cheaply

Blackman notes the repeated visual habits: the same shop NPC, the same wheel, the
same shot of the condo, and in the penny series a set of daily rituals. Repetition
of a motif reads as familiar, and familiar is comfortable.

Applied here: on a single short this is nearly free and mostly matters across a
lane. The same opening frame treatment, the same end card, the same sound. It is
what makes three videos read as one channel rather than three experiments.

## Nothing is taken before something is given

Haire's point about the piggy bank: Trahan makes almost no withdrawal until
substantial value has landed, with only a brief mention around a third of the way
in. The only way to get anything from an audience is to give more than you take.

Applied here: no follow prompt, no link, no pitch before the payoff. The end card
is the withdrawal and it comes last. A short that asks for a follow at second
three has taken before it gave.

## Compressing 20 minutes into 30 seconds

The sources are all long-form. This is the translation, and it is the part that
matters, because the techniques do not survive being copied at length.

A 30-second short gets exactly this:

```
hook          1 beat    the tested line from role 2, verbatim
context       1 line    the rules, the constraint, why care
escalation    3 beats   each raises the stakes, each changes the situation
payoff        1 beat    the withheld result, at the end
breath        ~1s       one moment to let it land
```

What gets dropped and why:

- **Characters and arcs.** No room. The person is the only character.
- **Multiple disruptions.** One, at the point attention would otherwise drift,
  which on a 30-second short is around the 12 to 15 second mark.
- **The moral turn.** Lovely at 20 minutes, pretentious at 30 seconds.
- **Cliffhangers.** Only if the lane genuinely runs a series.

What must survive, or the structure is pointless:

- The constraint, or there is no tension.
- The withheld payoff at the end, or there is no reason to reach it.
- Escalation, or it is a list.

## The word budget

Set it here, before a word of script exists, from the target length. This is the
gate the whole skill turns on: the renderer derives duration from the last spoken
word, so the script is the timeline.

| Target | Words at `+8%` | Shape |
|---|---|---|
| 15s | 45-50 | hook + one beat + payoff |
| 30s | 90-100 | hook + context + three beats + payoff |
| 45s | 135-150 | as above with a turn before the payoff |
| 60s | 180-200 | ceiling; retention falls off past here |

edge-tts at `+8%` speaks about 3.1 words a second. Count words, do not estimate
seconds.

Divide the budget across the beats and write the number beside each one. Role 4
writes to those numbers.
