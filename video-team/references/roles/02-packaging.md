# Role 2 — Packaging

**Method source:** Paddy Galloway's public strategy work. Primary source for this
file is his long interview with Colin and Samir, "The New Rules of YouTube"
(`colinandsamir.com/resources/the-new-rules-of-youtube-from-paddy-galloway`),
plus his own posts on rapid channel growth and his 12-month channel plan as
summarised at `youtube.com/watch?v=SYbQqMeq48o`. He has worked on videos totalling
billions of views and describes his own job as view maximisation.

**Produces:** `hooks[]` (five, ranked) and the first frame.

Method, not voice. Same rule as role 1.

---

## The premise: it is a click-and-watch platform

His central argument is that the platform is not really a video platform, it is a
series of decisions: the viewer decides whether to click, then whether to keep
watching. A great video nobody clicks is not a great video in any sense that
matters.

The leverage numbers he gives: small creators spend something like 95% of their
effort on filming and editing and about 5% on ideation and packaging, while top
creators put roughly 30% into ideation and packaging. His case study is the
astrophotographer Ian Lauer, who went from 2-3k views per video to over a million
by changing how videos were framed rather than how they were shot.

Applied here: this role gets real effort, not a line written on the way past. On
a 30-second short the "click" is the first frame and the first second, and
everything else in the chain is downstream of it.

## Specific beats vague, always

His Ian Lauer example is the clearest illustration: "I photographed the Milky
Way" became "Photographing the Milky Way in 10 minutes, 1 hour, and 24 hours".
Same footage. The second one has a visible structure, a comparison, and a
curiosity gap.

Applied here: interrogate every hook for a number, a constraint or a comparison
that could replace an adjective. "I made a website fast" is vague. "A full
website in the time it takes to make a coffee" has a unit.

## Familiar but unexpected

A video should be recognisable as belonging to its niche while carrying a twist
that makes someone stop. His examples include Johnny Harris's "$25K vs $25M",
which takes a well-worn three-tier comparison format and applies it to income.

Applied here: half the hook should be instantly legible to Byron's audience
(tradies, small business owners, people building with AI), and half should be the
thing they have not seen done to it.

## Steal formats across niches

Explicitly recommended. His case: Red Bull's "World's Fastest Drone vs F1 Car"
borrowing from car-channel drag-race formats like Carwow. Formats such as "$1 vs
$100" or "10 minutes vs 24 hours" transplant into new niches and feel fresh to an
audience that has not seen them.

This is the most practical idea in the file for Byron, because his niches are
under-formatted. "$1 vs $100" applied to websites, quotes, tools, or a trade's
equipment is a format his competitors are not running.

The curiosity-gap frameworks he names as consistently working:

| Framework | Shape |
|---|---|
| Superlative | the most, the worst, the largest, the only |
| Versus | A vs B, with a real winner |
| Three-tier | cheap vs mid vs absurd |
| Time-boxed | in 10 minutes, 1 hour, 24 hours |
| Unbelievable number | the specific figure that sounds wrong |

On that last one, his cited example of exaggeration done through clarity rather
than hype is Veritasium's "Why Are 96,000,000 Black Balls on This Reservoir?" —
the number is doing the work, and it is true.

## Write five, then three frames

From his 12-month plan: generate around 100 ideas a week, make three distinct
thumbnails per video, and study outlier content in your niche.

Compressed to one short: five hooks, and for the winner, three different first
frames rather than the first one that occurs to you. Vary the five by shape so
they are genuinely different bets, not five rewrites of one:

| Shape | Example |
|---|---|
| Stated stakes | "I gave six AI agents one idea and deleted my editor." |
| Open loop | "The fifth agent is the one nobody builds." |
| Contradiction | "The best AI video tool is six worse ones." |
| Blunt result | "One sentence in. Finished video out." |
| Direct address | "Your AI videos look the same because you use one model for six jobs." |

## Tiny tweaks compound

His Tim Gabe example: an old video's thumbnail improved by roughly 30-40% and the
video snowballed to about 40x the views per day. Small packaging changes compound
because the platform rewards what earns clicks and holds attention.

Applied here: a short that underperforms is worth re-hooking and re-rendering
before it is written off. The footage and the reel are cached in `work/`, so a
second hook is close to free. That is the cheapest experiment in the whole skill.

## The gates

Run all three, in order, on the top hook:

1. **Scroll stop.** Viewers see a title and thumbnail for far less time than you
   think. Does it stop the scroll before it is read properly?
2. **Sound off.** Most of the feed is muted. Does the first frame's text carry it
   alone? If it only lands when spoken, it is a script line, not a hook.
3. **Promise keeping.** Does the video actually deliver this? A hook the content
   does not pay off teaches the algorithm that people leave.

## The 80/20 rule, and predicting before upload

Two of his operating habits worth stealing:

- Aim for about 80% audience overlap on most videos, and reserve about 20% for
  experiments. A channel that experiments on everything has no audience; one that
  never experiments cannot find a new format.
- Predict how a video is expected to perform **before** it goes up, rather than
  panicking afterwards. He notes creators routinely panic over eight out of ten
  videos, which is normal variance rather than a crisis.

Applied here: write the expectation into the brief. When the short lands, the
question is whether it beat its own prediction, not whether it went viral.

## Ride it until they stop you

He is direct that when a format works you double down rather than abandoning it
because copycats appeared, comparing it to running the same play in American
football until the defence stops it.

**This contradicts role 1's source, and the contradiction is real.** The MrBeast
document says the opposite: ditch formats while they still work, because
audiences want new things and channels that rehash formats for years die, and
ideally never run two videos of the same format back to back.

Do not paper over it. They are optimising different things: one is protecting a
decade-old channel's freshness, the other is maximising views on a format that
is currently working. For Byron, at zero audience, **Galloway's side wins.** He
has no format equity to protect and no audience to bore. When something works,
run it until the numbers say stop. Revisit this the day a format is his
signature.

## Handoff

- `hooks[]` — five, each with its shape and one line of reasoning
- `winner` — which one, and why it beat the others
- `frames[]` — three first-frame concepts for the winner
- `expect` — the honest prediction for this video before it goes up
- `_rejected` — the four, kept so a re-run does not circle back to them
